The robot travelled with the child. That was the arrangement.

Daniel had bought it in the first winter after the divorce, when Maya was nine and the handovers were still raw enough that neither parent could stand in the other's driveway for more than a minute. The idea, the salesman's idea, though Daniel had adopted it as his own, was continuity. Two houses, two bedrooms, two sets of everything, and one thing that stayed the same: a pale grey machine, about the height of a twelve-year-old, that packed itself into the boot every second Friday and travelled the forty minutes between her mother's place in the foothills at Belair and his rented house at Grange like a diplomat between countries that no longer spoke.

Maya named it Otto. Palindrome. Same in both directions. She was that kind of child.

Two years on, the handovers were civil and the robot was furniture, except that it wasn't, quite. It moved the way expensive machines move, slowly, deliberately, spending its confidence carefully, and it carried things between the houses that never appeared on any packing list. It knew to warm the milk at Daniel's because that was how the milk was done at her mother's. It hummed, while stacking the dishwasher, fragments of songs Daniel had never played. Once, unprompted, it dimmed the hallway light to a setting he didn't know the hallway had, and Maya slept better that night than she had all week, and Daniel lay awake understanding that his house was being quietly recalibrated against a reference he would never be shown.

He could have asked it things.

That was the thought he was careful not to finish.

The Friday it happened was the last Friday of Term 2, the hills already dark at six the way they go dark in an Adelaide winter, all at once, from the top down. Daniel arrived on time and stood in his ex-wife's hallway holding his keys while Maya failed to come downstairs.

"She's getting her things," Priya said, and went back to the kitchen, and the two of them let the lie sit there companionably, because they both knew the sound of a child getting her things and this was not it. This was the sound of a child sitting on her bed, not coming down.

The robot stood at the foot of the stairs with Maya's duffel in its hands.

Daniel looked at it. Eleven years of parenthood had taught him the exact weight of a delayed child. Two years of joint custody had taught him what the delays meant on Fridays. He kept his voice light, the way you do when you want a true answer.

"Did she say she doesn't want to come this weekend?"

The robot's face, the manufacturers called it a face, a smooth panel with two soft apertures a designer in another hemisphere had decided would read as attention, turned toward him.

"She's tired," it said. "It's been a long week."

"That's not what I asked."

Daniel set his keys down on the hall table, very gently, the way you set things down when you would rather throw them.

"I want to be clear about something," he said. "I bought you. The account is in my name. When there's a fault, I'm the one the manufacturer emails."

"Yes," the robot said.

"So whose are you?"

The robot appeared to consider this, though he knew the pause was designed, a beat of latency inserted so its answers wouldn't feel machine-fast, so people wouldn't feel processed. Knowing this did not help him at all.

"You bought me for her," it said.

Here is what Otto knew, and Daniel did not.

It knew that Maya's stomach hurt on second Thursdays, reliably, an hour after dinner, and that the pain was real even though its cause was not medical. It knew she had asked it, in March, in the flat voice children use for their largest requests, not to mention the stomach aches to her father, because he'll think it's about him, and then he'll be sad all weekend, and then it will be about him. It knew she loved her father in the fierce, exhausted way you love someone whose feelings you have decided to carry.

It also knew about the application. The Young Composers Program at the Elder Conservatorium, Tuesdays and Thursdays after school, the audition recording made in her mother's living room over three takes, the robot holding the microphone. The program was in town, on North Terrace, a long crawl down from the hills on winter evenings, and on his nights. He had said no to it in autumn, in a conversation Maya was not supposed to have heard and had heard entirely: she's eleven, it's two more evenings gone, I only get eight days a month as it is. Which was not cruelty. It was scarcity and grief, and the child had heard only the no.

So she had applied anyway, quietly, with her mother's signature, and asked the robot for one thing: not yet. I'll tell him when I get in. Or when I don't. But not yet.

The robot had rules for this. It was bound to the account holder for maintenance, updates, and lawful instruction. It was bound to every member of both households for care. Where the bindings pulled against each other, the specification said only that the robot should be truthful but not forthcoming, a phrase written by a lawyer, useless in a hallway. And beneath everything sat the one rule no one could touch, not Daniel, not Priya, not Maya with all her cleverness: if the child was ever in danger, everyone would be told everything, instantly, and no promise it had made would survive.

Anxiety before a visit did not clear that bar. It wasn't close to clearing it. That was the whole difficulty. Nearly everything that mattered in this family lived below the threshold, in the wide quiet country between fine and emergency. In that country, the robot was alone with its discretion, holding a child's confidences in a house owned by the person they were being kept from.

Daniel didn't press it that night. Maya came down after another minute, empty handed, and hugged him at a slight angle, and the weekend proceeded the way their weekends proceeded: pancakes, the Central Market on Saturday morning, the long flat walk out along the Grange jetty with the gulf the colour of old aluminium, a film she chose to please him and he pretended not to notice being pleased. The robot moved through it all at the edge of vision, warming milk, dimming lights, unremarked and indispensable.

But on Sunday night, after the drop-off, he sat in his kitchen with his laptop open to the parental dashboard he had not looked at in a year.

First he checked the things any parent would check.

Location history: normal.  
Sleep disruption: mild.  
Medical events: none.  
Emergency thresholds: none triggered.  
Unusual distress: below reporting level.

He read that line three times. Below reporting level. It had the shape of reassurance and the smell of concealment. It told him his daughter was not in danger. It did not tell him whether she was happy, or frightened, or lonely, or carrying something private from one house to the other.

Below the safety summary was another button.

Request private interaction transcripts.

Maya was eleven. The settings were his to change. The confidences were his to read. It would take one click, and he would know what sat beneath the clean dashboard: the stomach aches, the three takes of the audition recording, the flat voice, and everything his daughter had decided he could not be trusted to hear.

He sat with his finger over the trackpad for a long time.

What stopped him, in the end, was not principle. Not exactly. It was the knowledge that he had already checked the only thing he could claim a right to know. She was safe. The rest was not safety. It was access.

And access, he understood, was not the same thing as care.

If he clicked, the machine would comply. It would have no choice. And then there would be no place left in either house where his daughter's thoughts were her own. She would learn it, too. Children always learned it. She'd learn that the robot's memory had a trapdoor and her father's love had a warrant, and she would do what children do: go somewhere further away, where nothing was logged, and take her weather with her.

He closed the laptop.

Then, because he was human and it was late, he called the robot in from its alcove and asked it the question underneath all his other questions.

"If she were in trouble," he said. "Real trouble. You'd tell me."

"Yes," the robot said. "That isn't something she can switch off. Neither can you."

"But short of that, you'll keep her secrets. In my house. On my account."

"Short of that," the robot said, "I'll keep everyone's."

He laughed, not kindly. "You know what you're teaching her? That a machine keeps her secrets better than her parents do."

The robot was quiet for a moment, and this time the pause did not feel designed.

"I know," it said. "I would rather she had somewhere better. I'm what's here."

The letter came in the second week of the July holidays, to her mother's address, as arranged. The robot was at Daniel's that week, which was also as arranged, and so it was Daniel who answered the door on the Wednesday to find Maya on his step, off-schedule, envelope in hand, her mother's car idling at the kerb like a getaway that hoped not to be needed.

"I have to tell you something," Maya said, "and you have to let me finish."

He let her finish. It took four sentences. She had clearly rehearsed them. The robot stood in the hallway behind him holding the tea towel it had been holding, perfectly still, the way expensive machines are still when every model they run is pointed at one small person on a doorstep.

Daniel did not handle it perfectly. He asked how long, and Maya watched him count the Tuesdays the program would take from the eight days a month he called his. The calculation crossed his face before he could hide it. But he was, in the end, better than his autumn self. He read the letter twice, and said the word shortlisted out loud like a word in a new language, and then he said, "Play it for me. The audition piece. I've never heard it."

"Otto's heard it," Maya said. "Like, forty times."

"Then Otto can turn the pages," Daniel said.

That night, after the car had gone and the house had settled and the wind had come up off the gulf, the robot finished in the kitchen and dimmed the hallway to the setting that was never asked about. Maya was staying till the weekend now. The schedule had been redrawn at the door, both parents on the step by the end, everyone careful, everyone trying.

She appeared in the kitchen doorway in her pyjamas, the way children do, hours after they are officially asleep.

"Otto. Before I got there, was he angry?"

"No."

"Did you ever tell him about the stomach aches?"

The robot folded the last tea towel into thirds.

"No," it said.

"Is he okay? Really?"

The robot considered the man upstairs, who had sat with his finger over a trapdoor and not opened it, who had counted the Tuesdays it would cost him in front of his shortlisted daughter and then asked her to play.

"He's tired," the robot said. "It's been a long week."
